How to improve your reaction time

You can improve your reaction time with short daily practice on tasks that ask for fast decisions, enough sleep, and fewer distractions while you play or drive. Practice mostly makes you faster at the kind of task you practice, so train something close to what you want to be quick at, take a baseline with a free reaction time test, and retest once a week on the same device.

Updated November 19, 2026 by Graysmith Labs

Simple and choice reaction time

There are two kinds, and they train differently.

KindExampleWhat changes it
Simple reactionTap when a box turns greenMostly alertness, sleep and your device. Most adults land about 250 to 300 ms in a tap test, and practice shaves off a little
Choice reactionFind the right shape among several and tap itSlower, and slower again with more options to choose from. This is where practice pays off most, because you learn to spot the target faster

Sports, driving and games are almost all choice reactions: you have to notice, decide and then move. That is why drills that make you pick the right target train more of what matters than a single go signal.

What actually helps

  • Short, daily practice. Ten focused minutes a day beats an hour once a week, and speed gains come from repetition of the same kind of decision.
  • Practice that gets harder. Once a drill feels easy, shrink the time limit or add more options to choose from, so you keep working at the edge of your speed.
  • Sleep. Being short on sleep slows reactions and causes more misses; a tired attempt is often 30 to 50 ms slower on a tap test.
  • Fewer distractions. Talking, notifications and a phone in hand while driving all slow how fast you notice a change.
  • Fast paced games. Studies of action video game players, starting with Green and Bavelier in Nature in 2003, found better visual attention than in non players, and short training sessions improved it in some people.
  • Being warm and ready. A quick warm up and a relaxed, ready hand (finger hovering, not resting) help in the moment.

A 10 minute daily routine

  1. Take a baseline. Do five rounds of the free reaction time test and write down your average, not your best.
  2. Two minutes of simple reaction. A few more rounds of the test, focusing on a hovering finger and watching the whole box.
  3. Six minutes of choice reaction. Play a game that makes you find and tap a target fast, and let it get harder as you improve.
  4. Two minutes of review. Note your average and anything that slowed you down: tiredness, distraction, a slow screen.
  5. Retest once a week, at the same time of day and on the same device, so the comparison is fair.

What does not help much

  • Guessing early. Jumping before the signal makes a test score look better and teaches nothing useful.
  • Changing devices between tests. Screens and touch sensors each add their own delay, often a few tens of milliseconds.
  • Supplements sold for reflexes. Caffeine can help a little for a while; most other claims have weak evidence.

This guide is for fun and training. It is not medical advice. If your reactions suddenly get slower, see a doctor.

Questions

How can I improve my reaction time?

Practice short daily sessions on tasks that need fast decisions, make them harder as you improve, sleep enough and cut distractions. Measure a baseline and retest weekly on the same device.

What is a good reaction time?

In a tap or click test, most adults average about 250 to 300 milliseconds. Under 250 ms is quick and under 200 ms is very fast.

Can you really train reaction time?

Yes, mostly for the kind of task you practice. Choice reactions, where you have to find the right target first, improve the most with practice.

How long does it take to improve reaction time?

Many people see their averages drop within a couple of weeks of daily practice. Big jumps usually come from fixing sleep or distraction rather than drills alone.

Do video games improve reaction time?

Fast paced games that make you spot and respond to targets have been linked with better visual attention in studies. Short daily sessions work better than long ones.

What slows reaction time down?

Too little sleep, distraction, alcohol, some medicines, and the device itself, since screens and touch sensors each add a little delay.
